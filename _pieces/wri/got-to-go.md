The right brake lever dangled almost out of reach of his left forefinger, 

but by leaning forward at the waist and twisting his shoulder, Jon managed to 

reach around the frame and toggle the el bracket against the tire of the 

wheelchair. Nancy didn’t like him using the commode by himself, but he was 

not a child, who needed to ask for help going to the bathroom. Actually, 

because the wheelchair couldn’t fit in any of the bathrooms, the commode 

chair stood at the side of the guestroom, in front of the desk, beside the single 

bed where he slept since the accident, the room’s location right off the main 

hall all the more reason to keep going to relieve himself to himself. She was 

unloading and loading laundry into the washer and the dryer, but she paid 

close attention to any possibility of him falling, so it was important that he be 

careful and methodical, and silent. First step, he locked the wheels before 

trying to stand and, even more so, before sitting down, because the 

wheelchair tends to roll backwards and leave him on the floor, no place to 

end up in his condition. Next, with his left hand, he lifted his right knee and 

freed the foot from its resting place on the footrest, released the catch on the 

frame, and swung the stirrup assembly sideways out of the way. Here came 

the hard part, he leaned forward with the left fingers gripping the edge of the 

desk, and raised himself into a standing position, taking the full weight of his 

body with the left leg, the good one. Now the tricky part, balancing on the 

right leg and left hand, the heel of the palm taking most of the force, slid and 

pivoted the left foot backwards ninety degrees, while turning the hips and 

torso, and then swung the numb foot into alignment parallel with its opposite. 

At this point, he stood back-on to the commode, wheelchair on his right, so 

far so good. He pulled down the elastic waistband of the workout pants, 

lowering one-handed stepwise along his thighs, to just above the knees, no 

 
farther, or he won’t be able reach the pants when he gets up. Bending into a 

half squat, he angled his right forearm across his lap, so the idle hand won’t 

get caught underneath him when he sits, and then, grasping the tubular arm of 

the chair, he lowered himself slowly towards the seat, feeling for the center 

of the hole.  With a half inch to go on his descent, his legs gave out, and he 

sat heavily, as the metal legs of the commode skittered slightly across the 

floor. “What are you doing in there?” Nancy called from the laundry room. 

“Who can tell me how to pronounce this word?” Mrs. Rutherford 

turned from the blackboard to face the class. Ellen O’Conner, second from 

front, teacher’s pet, raised her arm straight up, as she always did. Jonathon, in 

last row because he was tall and his last name started with W, vaguely made 

out the cursive letters, p-r-e-s-e-n-t, but he was used to guessing the meaning 

of the blurry smudges written at the front of the classroom, after all, it was 

just a small puzzle of the greater mystery of school, things that came easy to 

other kids, but eluded Jonathon. But although he could clearly hear the sound 

the word made inside his head, the idea of raising his hand didn’t occur to 

him, or rather, it occurred to him much like ideas of flying, or being popular, 

that is, in his dreams and fantasies.  Typically, Mrs. Rutherford took pity and 

didn’t call on him, but occasionally, out of a mean mood, or sense of fair 

play, she said “Jonathon?” Hands in his lap, head bowed as if prayer, he 

contorted his lips and clenched his jaw and repeated “P…P…P…” over and 

over, about once a second, like a dying fish caught in the bottom of the boat. 

The other students knew not to giggle or stare, under threat of Mrs. 

Rutherford’s punishment, but that was somehow worse, looking down at their 

books, fiddling with their pencils, thinking of Porky Pig cartoons, while they 

waited for the teacher to say, “All right, Jonathon,” and call on another one of 

their classmates. Jonathon understood there were kids in wheelchairs who 

couldn’t walk, and kids with white canes who couldn’t see, but there were 

none of those in his school, and he couldn’t imagine them being required to 

play dodge ball in gym class. And follow the rules about going to the 

bathroom either. The student had to raise his hand, and when called upon by 

the teacher, ask “May I please go to the restroom?” then when given 

permission, go up to the teacher’s desk to get the black wooden hall pass (the 

white one was for going the office), and then and only then, proceed out the 

door and down the hall to the boy’s room. Raising his hand to say that 

particular thing, more than any other, except possibly his name, was even 

harder than pronouncing each letter of an unknown word in a spelling bee in 

front of the whole school, so he learned to hold it in until recess or lunchtime 

or dismissal, even when he couldn’t hold it in anymore. He desperately 

wanted to avoid the abject humiliation and total ostracization of last year 

when Mrs. Eiten, that year’s teacher, leaned over his desk and, in a hoarse 

whisper audible in the far corners of the classroom, as the puddle under his 

chair turned the brown and yellow striated floor tiles to gold, said, 

“Jonathon? Did you have an accident?” 

“No. Fine. Bathroom,” Jon said, his voice loud and hoarse, but not 

pissed off, he hoped, as the words left his throat. No point in making Nancy 

mad at his irritation, she’d just say she was only being concerned.  Now the 

pee wouldn’t start, he hated sitting down like a girl, it was constricting for a 

man. Relax. Deep breaths, think about something else, otherwise he’d right 

back here in fifteen minutes, what would Nancy think then, is there 

something wrong, maybe you should have a urologist check you out, maybe 

your bladder is retaining urine. No thanks. Thank god he’s out of the hospital. 

He remembered waking up with a plastic tube coming out from where he 

never imagined anything was meant to go in, leading to a clear plastic bag, 

Ziploc freezer size, half full of the color of weak iced tea slung from the 

bedrail. He overheard Nancy complaining to the doctor about the resident 

who put in the catheter when Jon was admitted, unconscious, Nancy said the 

resident kept trying to force it in, a couple inches in and out, four or five 

times, even after the nurse told her to stop. As if he didn’t have the enlarged 

prostate problems typical to his age, when they withdrew the catheter the 

next day, as part of preparing him for discharge to an acute care facility, the 

urge to void came every half hour, agonizing, clenching his otherwise lifeless 

right arm across his chest. What came out, after Jon moaned enough to gain 

somebody’s attention to pull down the bed clothes, lift up the hospital gown, 

and position the translucent male urinal, resembling an empty one-quart 

plastic milk bottle but with an oversized round mouth offset at an angle, was 

a dribble, sometimes a trickle, barely enough to yellow the corner where the 

bottom met the side of the receptacle. After a couple minutes, the spasm 

subsided, Jon waved away the urinal with his left, his working, hand, and 

opened his eyes, maintaining eye contact only with the ceiling, while his 

modesty was reassembled, and everything returned to abnormal. At least he 

was familiar with not being able to speak a word, although in this case, the 

cause was a severed connection between thought and mouth, an apraxia due 

to his stroke, rather a seizure in the mechanism of pronunciation, his stutter 

from his childhood. Oh, stuck mute again. Jon thought that people get used to 

pretty much anything, lying there in the humming room unable to move, 

reduced to grunting like a monkey when he had to pee, depending on the 

indifference of strangers to keep him fed and hydrated and medicated and not 

filthy. At last, Jon felt the stream release and heard it splatter hollowly into 

the bucket hung from the seat of the commode, sounding like a downspout in 

an intermittent drizzle. He returned to trying to imagine how to pull up his 

pants and get back into the wheelchair, and get through the congealing 

afternoon by drowsing over the day’s reincarnate headlines, and get over his 

resentment over Nancy’s kindly forbearance and his own ossifying 

incompetence and the prospect of living the rest of his life this way.
