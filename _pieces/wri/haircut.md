I got a haircut on the dock, 
scissors scrunching one year’s growth beside my ear, 
errant strands whispering in the tepid southwest breeze 
in the bitter blue September solstice morn. 
Nearly a year ago, my full-grown son cut the braid 
that I had kept for four decades until 
a stroke of bad luck, a bolt from the blue, 
lay me in the hospital. 
Lying there then, I counted on two good hands by now 
to at least tie it back, but instead this day I asked him 
to bring down the sharp scissors, a worn towel, 
and an old door-back mirror with loose wooden frame. 
He propped the mirror against a rubber-capped pipe on the dock, 
aligned with my deck chair, angling mostly blue sky 
and a sliver of lake and shoreline at the bottom. 
With my good hand clenching the towel at my neck, 
shirtless, the sag from lost chest muscle mass concealed, 
the reflection inverting right for left my inert crippled arm, 
I sat still while the taut torso of my heir snipped around me. 
Mouse nests drifted on my lap and onto the warped boards below, 
clumps of gray and dirty blond caught on the raised grain. 
Shorn, people tell me I’m lucky to stand and step, 
I brushed myself off, when a random gust lifted the mirror from its prop, 
stood the image upright for a quivering moment, and then sent it on its way. 
The accelerating frame wiped across sky, trees, water, chair, 
and the rapidly growing dark-gapped planks, 
until, with a thunderclap, it fell flat on its brown paper back. 
Before it hit me about my game leg, my limp arm, 
I started forward to bend down to pick up the fallen glass, 
but drew up short when my son said, 
“I’ll get it, dad.” 
He turned over the mirror on the dock, 
frame flexing at the corner with the crunch of a brittle ice cube tray, 
while the sky shattered into shards of picture-puzzle blue. 
In a flash, everything changed, jagged fragments, 
broken brain.
