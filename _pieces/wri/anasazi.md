Clifton lay on his back on the mat table. “When do you think I can start 

playing golf?” 

“Breathe.” Rudi depressed the shoulder farther into the socket. 

“It depends. The extent of your injury. Your commitment to therapy. 

How you follow the home program. Learning how your body works. 

Visualizing your goal. Everything is possible. It’s all a matter time.” 

“Oh, that’s odd,” Clifton smiled. “I couldn’t play golf at all before  

my stroke. What do you think my handicap would be? Holding the club  

one-handed? Or getting in and out of the cart?” 

Last time Clifton played full-sized golf was on a family vacation 

when the boys were little at a resort in Sedona, one of those three-night 

all-inclusive packages. The resort straddled a dry creek bed, ringed 

by red sandstone bluffs, and, in the flat bottomland beside the entrance road, 

included a nine-hole par-three pitch-and-putt course. Clifton and the kids 

signed out putters, short irons, and golf balls from the sports center,  

and then walked down the road to the first hole, the well-irrigated grass 

an unlikely green against red rock under blue sky. Off the third tee,  

Clifton badly sliced the ball across the road, into the drainage ditch beyond, 

and went to retrieve it. 

“Play it as it lays!” A man walked up the road toward them. 

“What?” Clifton looked at the man, fit, khaki slacks, white polo. 

“The rule is, play it as it lays.” The man approached. Clifton shrugged, 

bent down to pick up his ball from the red dirt. 

“A fine example you’re setting for your children!” The man stopped, 

hands in his pockets, massive silver-colored watch on his tan wrist. 

“You playing this game?” Clifton straightened up, holding his clubs 

 
in his left hand out in front of him, standing in the ditch slightly below 

the man’s level on the pavement above.  At this angle, the man’s jutting 

cleft chin shadowed his prominent Adam’s apple, chest hairs filling 

his open shirt collar. Clifton took a step out of the ditch, brandishing 

the club heads a little. 

“Rules are rules.” The man said over his shoulder as he walked away. 

“What did he want?’ The oldest son, Paul, asked his father 

when he crossed to back the fairway. Clifton turned to watch the man’s 

diminishing silhouette heading to the clubhouse below the red ridgeline. 

“Never mind.” Clifton centered his ball on the dimpled rubber mat.  

“Stay with me here.” Rudi held the arm straight overhead and 

with his other hand beneath Clifton’s back and began to separate the muscles 

of his shoulder blade from his rib cage. Clifton tensed with a sharp intake 

of breath. “Relax,” said Rudi. 

On that same vacation, the family took a day trip to a state park 

that encompassed several sites of ruins once inhabited by cliff-dwellers, 

ancestors of the Pueblo Indians, commonly called the Anasazi or “old ones”, 

meaning “ancestors of enemies” in the Navajo language. They followed 

the park ranger up the modern wooden staircase from the floor of the box canyon 

to stacked cubical chambers of stone and adobe built into the sheer side walls. 

Aaron entered a doorway, Clifton followed him into small low-ceilinged room. 

“You can’t even stand up in here, Dad,” His younger son, Aaron, said. 

“The adults were not much taller than your boy here,” said the ranger. 

“Life expectancy about thirty-five years, given the tuberculosis, exacerbated 

the constant smoke from the indoor cooking fires. That, and the warfare.”   

Clifton bent his neck to examine the blackened tree limbs supporting 

the clay floor of the upper story. “What happened to them?” 

“Two hundred year drought, starting in the late thirteenth century. 

 
 
 
 
 
 
Wiped out the entire populations of the Anasazi sites.” The ranger  

went on to explain how the cliff-dwellers most likely tried migrate 

to where water was more plentiful, but the canyons and river floodplains 

were already inhabited by other tribes, who defended their territories. 

Those who weren’t killed in the fighting, starved to death. 

On the way back to the hotel driving the car, Clifton looked out 

at the high desert plain, stony sand and rocky outcroppings, stunted 

sparse bushes and scrub. He thought about what he read on the effects 

of elevated CO2 levels in the air as the result of burning coal and oil, 

melting glaciers, rising oceans, stronger storms, the drying out of 

temperate regions, expanding deserts. Underwater cities, abandoned 

towns, uninhabitable dustbowls. Mass migrations, famines, epidemics, 

widespread warfare. Clifton didn’t expect to live long enough to see 

the worst of it. But his children. Quiet dark-eyed Paul. Fair and 

eager Aaron. 

“Raise your other arm over your head, I’ll support 

this one,” Rudi said. Clifton did what he was told, arching his back  

and attempting to pull down on his shoulder blades, but as Rudi extended 

his arm to nearly horizontal past his ear, he felt as if his shoulder 

was being dislocated. “Breathe,” said Rudi. 

Maybe his kids would be okay, joining the exodus of first-world 

citizens to the new cities built above the arctic circle later on in this century, 

if they had the degrees and skills and experience to make them sufficiently 

valuable to the wealthy and powerful elite who controlled immigration. 

He couldn’t picture them surviving by foraging and fighting and breeding 

in a post-apocalyptic free-for-all world. Much like he couldn’t imagine 

himself living on his own after his stroke. Aaron did come to see him 

in the hospital for a few days. But then he had to get back to help plant 

 
 
to the winter crop at the organic farm that employed him part-time, 

and back to his live-in girlfriend that Clifton had never met, he said she did 

bodywork, not the automotive kind apparently. All well and good 

Aaron trying to save the planet by living sustainably, but if the rest 

humanity destroyed the planet by living destructively, what good 

would it do to sacrifice himself? The older son, Paul, didn’t come home 

anymore. At last contact, he hinted that he was on the watchlist, so Clifton 

suspected he and his anarchist hacker friends were caught in the data sieves 

of federal intelligence agencies. Great, just the kind of young people 

the authorities want to keep around, people trying to overthrow 

the government and the world financial system.  Perhaps his computer 

expertise would help at his parole hearing, fat chance of that, 

Paul always was serious and stubborn, a true believer. Maybe  

Clifton should have raised the boys differently, less accommodating 

to their neglecting homework and chores, less tolerant of their backtalk 

and cruelty, less forgiving of their carelessness, more like how 

his own father raised him. 

Rudi raised Clifton’s arm up to vertical, let it rest for half of a minute, 

then lowered it sideways across his body. “Let me pick the arm back up, 

you lower it as much as you can in this direction.” Clifton eased his shoulder 

towards his neck slowly, ready for the onset of the pain he was sure 

awaited him when his elbow passed his chin. Sure enough, his shoulder 

cleaved, like razor wire inside drawn against the muscle, the tendons, 

and he tried to stop his arm’s further descent, but Rudi intervened, and 

forced it down another inch. “That’s it, that’s better,” Rudi said. 

Clifton closed his eyes. His old man was definitely old school, 

Old Testament, patriarchal, authoritarian, spare the rod, self-righteous.  

The kind of father that initially inspires fear in the son, later hatred, 

 
and eventually the desperate motivation to prove the father wrong 

about the son by becoming more successful than either of them could 

possibly have imagined. In Clifton’s case, he went from fat and lonely boy 

(“I’ll give you something to cry about”), to eager-to-please adolescent 

(“What’s your excuse this time”), to rebellious teenager (“You’re not 

leaving this house tonight”), to student of the arts (“How do you intend 

to support yourself”), to young entrepreneur (“Stop wasting your time. 

Get a good job with a good company”), to finally estranged from his father 

(silence). Clifton’s own parenting style, in reaction to how he was brought up, 

avoided hitting, yelling, humiliation, and more or less any criticism 

whatsoever directed at his children, rather, he tried to support them 

in whatever they wanted to do. Because they were denied the opportunity 

to rebel against their father, he thought, they were forced to turn against 

the social order, and because he invariably praised their routine progress 

as typical children, as adults they had no drive to succeed in the world. 

In spite of his low self esteem and chronic depression, both legacies 

from his father, Clifton achieved enviable stature and wealth in his industry. 

After years of not speaking to each other, he gave in to the popular wisdom 

about families letting bygones be bygones and the importance his teenagers  

knowing their grandfather. Clifton called his father, and arranged 

to have him come visit. The encounter played out amiably and superficially 

enough, like prizefighters warily circling one another around the ring 

in the opening round of a rematch. On the last day of the visit, when 

Clifton’s dread and trepidation had just about given in to relief, because 

all parties had maintained the proper distance, the old man drew him aside 

and said “I just can’t tell how proud  I am of you, your beautiful home, 

your lovely wife, Aaron and Paul growing up to be such fine young men, 

how successful you’ve been in your career, it’s unbelievable.” 

Clifton inflated with childish pleasure, until the elderly man concluded, 

“You know your mother and I pray for you every day that you will accept  

Jesus Christ into your heart as your personal savior.” Clifton smiled, but 

inwardly, he realized nothing had changed, he was still a unrepentant sinner 

in his father’s eyes, everything he had done and become didn’t matter. Good  

thing his father died a few years before Clifton’s stroke, God knows what 

he would tell his son after that, your stroke’s a blessing, you’ve been given 

the opportunity repent for your sins and ask forgiveness before 

the final judgment casts into eternal damnation.  

“Now lift your arm past your head, right past your ear. Don’t let it 

go off to one side.” Rudi supported the arm at a 45 degree angle out in front 

of Clifton’s waist. Clifton concentrated on lifting the arm, fighting against its  

almost willful tendency to deviate sideways, as if his arm was made of iron  

and the mat table was a gigantic magnet. “Keep it straight, right past the ear.” 

Clifton contemplated his sins. Better to believe he was somehow 

at fault than to accept the inescapable randomness of the indifferent universe. 

Even the doctors with all their diagnostic imaging had no clue, his health 

was excellent, he had none of the usual risks factors. Only his misspent 

youth, sedentary, smoking, drinking, using drugs, junk food diet, 

decades ago offered a possible explanation. Maybe he cleaned up 

his act too late, cleaned out his arteries too late. That, and his failure 

to honor his father by believing in Jesus. However, he should be grateful 

to divine providence, or blind chance, that he was not as bad off 

as some other stroke victims, bedridden, in nursing homes, unable to talk, 

waiting for somebody to come to re-dress their bedsores, waiting to die. 

On the other hand, he didn’t feel particularly lucky, why should he 

not be one of those men living hale and hearty into an advance age, 

despite not having taken care of themselves at all. It’s not fair. 

At least believing in heaven and hell after death holds out the hope 

for some kind of justice. All Clifton has to hope for is recovering some  

part of his ability to walk and talk and use his right his hand again like 

a normal person, like a husband, a father, a man, not a good-for-nothing 

cripple, dependent on the kindness of his wife and the people she hires 

to take to care of him, therapists like Rudi. Other clients at this place, 

when he asks them how they’ve been coming here and what progress 

they’ve made, answer vaguely in numbers of years, and “It’s slow.” 

And they are ones who have stayed with it, not dropped out because 

they ran out of patience, or hope, or money. 

“Do you think I’ll ever get my arm back?” Clifton asked. 

“I do. Your shoulder is coming along real good. It’s all a matter 

of time,” Rudi said as he rotated Clifton’s arm in a wide half circle,  

outward from the shoulder, down almost to the hip, and then helped Clifton 

to raise it vertically overhead, over and around again. 

This method of working of his right arm reminded Clifton of when, after 

high school, Clifton and two friends drove cross-country in a ’62 VW Microbus. 

Somewhere in the Great Plains, the bus got to where it wouldn’t start, so Mike 

(it was his bus, and he usually drove), who couldn’t afford to have it fixed, 

parked it on flat ground or facing uphill, and when it was time to leave, 

Mike would climb in, while Cliff (as he was known then) and Don 

(the third friend) would push on the front end, beside the large chrome VW circle, 

rolling the bus backwards. Mike kept the clutch disengaged and the gearshift  

in reverse, and when the proper speed was attained, would pop the clutch and  

gun it. Then one of two things would happen: either the engine would rattle to life 

with a roar and the bus would speed backwards, or Don and Cliff would be 

brought up short as the bus reached the limits of its cylinders’ compression, 

the bus would halt, and a puff of smoke backfired from its tailpipe. Similarly, 

 
 
with the assistance of Rudi or another physical therapist, how Clifton tries 

to move the impacted (the term he has learned to use instead of bad) arm,   

thereby teaching other parts of his brain to take control of the movement 

from the dead brain parts which used to control the limb before his stroke. 

Sort of like turning the wheels on the bus round and round, and thereby 

through the bearings and axle and differential and transmission, 

rebuilding the engine. Or reconfigurating the backseat into a new engine. 

Admittedly, the brain is considerably more sophisticated than a 1200cc 

air-cooled VW engine, but even so, this approach takes a very long time, 

with the outcome uncertain. Nonetheless, Clifton feels that he has no 

alternative but to keep hoping that, standing in the failing light of dusk 

on a cold roadside turnout along a deserted highway somewhere in 

South Dakota, his broken down old bus will eventually start again.
