Also-ran 

Tim Van Hook 

“Death rates rising for middle-aged white Americans” 

The New York Times, November 2, 2015 

Tom Ashton lived next door to me in the house owned by his elderly mother, 

first house built on the cul-de-sac after the town platted out an former estate, 

mid-century modern, shallowly sloped gravel roof, a lot of glass. 

Most late mornings, he would crank up the old pickup truck in the driveway, 

one of those US-made whose front-end looks like a coffin, massive and molded, 

with one door red and the hood primed in contrast to the faded gold paint, 

windshield diagonally cracked at one corner, Vietnam vet plates, 

and drive to the convenience store at the corner for cigarettes and beer. 

Standing by the mailbox on the street, he seemed a friendly enough guy, 

returning a wave of greeting, answering not bad when asked how’re you doing,  

stocky grenade-shaped torso with low-slung jeans and a chambray work shirt, 

wispy fu Manchu moustache, thinning shoulder-length reddish hair, 

aviator glasses shielding heavy-lidded eyes, capillaries broken in his cheeks. 

One year, after the early winter rains washed the dust off Tom’s truck, 

but couldn’t dislodge the fallen leaves from beneath the underbody 

that the backwash of the gardeners’ leaf blowers couldn’t reach,  

and the low noon sun topped the crown of oaks behind Mrs. Ashton’s house, 

I noticed that I hadn’t seen him around much lately. 

The following spring, I saw him sitting in the sun on the stoop in his wheelchair, 

home from the veterans administration hospital, blanket around his shoulders, 

right leg truncated by a bandage below the knee.  

Pausing midway up the front walkway, I said good to see you back and 

 
 
how’re you feeling and you’re looking good and how sorry I was. 

Tom replied, expressionless, “Well. I guess I’ve had my fun.” 

I nodded, curious what he meant by fun, perhaps self-medicating the aftermath 

of the war with alcohol and drugs, but I didn’t know him well enough to ask. 

In the fall, his brother flew in for the funeral, commercial airline pilot, 

clean cut, the right stuff, with his young South American wife, and said, 

after Tom went back into the hospital and they had to take his other foot,   

they tried to interest him in his rehabilitation, but he would have none of it. 

They told the brother that Tom’s heart stopped in his sleep. 

The other driver ran the stop sign, but I didn’t see him until the instant 

before he T-boned the left side of my car and pushed me sidewise 

against the curb, where I sat for what seemed to be a long time, 

elated to be alive, while the adrenaline rush ebbed and I surveyed the damage. 

Much like the experience of my stroke, but speeded up,  

in which the left side of my brain was impacted, my body was ambulanced 

to the hospital, where I lay supine for what seemed to be a long time, 

bemused to be alive, and I never saw it coming. 

But different, because leaning over the console with the armrest pressing  

against my ribs, I had no doubt my life would continue much the same, 

unlike in bed with my head propped on pillows listening to the gray hum, 

I feared that my life had irretrievably changed for the worse. 

Later on, the fear was confirmed, when I realized the future held no quick fix 

at the body shop, I felt like a Nascar driver who hits the wall with a blown engine 

and drifts to the infield, out of the race, while all his competitors roar past, 

glad that they’re not me. 

Long ago, when I was trying to be patriotic, I bought an American pickup truck, 

but the engine seized up, apparently some obstruction in the lubrication, 

 
not long after the warrantee expired, so I traded it for my current car.  

Now the winter rains have started to wash the summer’s tree sap and pollen 

and road dust from the hood and roof into dark streaks dripping down the fenders, 

since I stopped driving a year ago, except to back it up in the driveway, 

because not only is changing gears tricky left- and one-handed with the stick shift, 

but my right foot can’t feel how much I’m pressing the gas or brake pedals. 

Also a year ago, I quit drinking, not that I didn’t miss it at first, but eventually 

I realized I didn’t need alcohol in my damaged brain to render me clumsy, 

off balance, slow of speech, and my words slurred any more than I was already. 

Decades ago I quit smoking, started running, changed my diet, because I wanted  

to live long enough to enjoy my retirement from the business, but these days  

I’m fresh out of fun. The stroke took away everything that gave me pleasure, 

staying in shape, building furniture, drawing, composing music, telling stories, 

reading, even my ability to write and talk and sing feels amputated. 

Let alone, for someone who prided himself on doing everything by himself, 

accepting my dependence on others for the trivial necessities of daily living. 

Tying my shoelaces. Cutting my fingernails. Twisting the caps of my medications. 

I continue with therapy, but after a few months of fairly rapid progress, 

the rehabilitation curve has flattened, and the spontaneous and easy gains are over. 

I can now glimpse the doorway of despair that leads people to give up on life, 

not the dread of the slow exit into old age and illness and disability and death, 

but the loss of hope that something, anything, will ever be good again, 

good enough to ease the shame of falling short, behind in the race, an also-ran, 

sidelined, finished, fated to be forever cast out of the garden. 

Most days these days, unlike Tom, I can convince myself to accept living 

like this for what’s left of my life, but I see his point.
