Clifton stopped in the driveway on way back from retrieving the mail 

at the street, shading his eyes with the envelopes in his hand, and looked up 

at the house, puzzled. Bits of sunshine danced on the north-facing cedar-shake roof, 

which slanted away from the near-solstice white sky, the corrugated thick ends 

of the shingles shadowed greenish-brown with dried-out last winter’s moss,  

the erratic bright spots above like glitter confetti reflecting the midday sun. 

From his position closer in on the lawn, the blobs resolved into winged bugs, 

darker against the sky over the roof ridge, larger than house flies, smaller 

than bumblebees, going and coming in tight parabolic arcs to and from 

midway up the roof. Wasps. What were yellow jackets doing up there?  

Clifton propped the stepladder below the gutter and climbed 

to the fourth rung to take a closer look. Eight feet in front of him, 

in the quarter-inch gap between two of the shake shingles, 

below the butt end of the shingle on the next course, wasps leisurely 

alighted and disappeared or emerged crawling and took wing 

from the tiny rectangular crevice, as if from a fingernail-sized open door. 

From his lookout above the eave, the roof extended like a choppy wooden sea, 

the sheen of the weathered grain on the split shakes glinting under the sun, 

the wasps taking off and landing like little toy fighter planes 

from the flight deck of a submerged miniature aircraft carrier. 

Or submarine hive. In past years Clifton found yellow jacket  

nests dug in the ground in remote parts of the yard, which he killed 

by pouring granular poison from a sack the gardener gave him 

down the nickel-sized entrance hole at first light, when the wasps 

were presumably sleeping, and then covering the hole with a brick. 

But never before on the roof, maybe they were escalating the conflict 

by attacking the house. The poured powdered poison couldn’t 

 
 
penetrate the crack between the shingles to kill them, and Clifton had no desire  

to tear off a section of the roof, especially to expose a nest of angry wasps. 

Early the next morning, Clifton returned to the ladder, this time 

armed with a can of wasp/hornet spray which (the can said) kills on contact, 

eliminates the nest, (and features) up to a 29-foot jet spray. He aimed 

the red nozzle at the entrance gap, from which at this hour only the isolated 

yellow jacket emerged, visible more by its motion than by its contrast 

to the dew-darkened shingles in the dawn, and depressed the button. 

A narrow pink stream shot out of the cap, and Clifton kept it trained on 

the opening, adjusting its sinusoidal curves like a scale-model day-glow  

focused fire-hose. Liquid ran down and blackened the lower and adjoining 

shakes, but surely some amount of poison must have penetrated the nest, 

given the almost edge-on angle up the slope at which he directed the jet.  

The sky had brightened appreciably and the dark stain had almost reached 

the gutter in front of him when the spray sputtered to a stop. A foamy 

residue lingered around the target, but no wasps crossed the threshold. 

Clifton waited for a couple minutes, then carrying the empty can, he climbed 

down the ladder. 

Inside the house later that day, Clifton happened to walk 

into what the family called the playroom, originally an attached garage 

converted to bonus room by the previous owners, when he noticed the stepladder 

outside the front window. He wondered if any of the liquid spray that ran down 

the roof could have dripped through to the interior. Scanning the ceiling, 

shallowly pitched during the remodel by attaching sheetrock directly on the rafters, 

he detected, within the smooth off-white expanse, a saucer-like blister, 

visible only by its downward curve brightened and its upward arc shadowed 

by the light from the window below. Picking up a broom handle, 

he poked at the center of the disc. With only the lightest touch, 

 
 
a jagged black hole tore through the curling white paint, as if  

the whole ceiling was painted on air, thin as two coats of latex paint. 

Expecting the worst, Clifton spread a plastic drop-cloth on the carpet, 

placed another can of wasp killer in easy reach, and enlarged the hole 

with the broomstick. A writhing mass of somnolent yellow jackets, 

gray papery hexagonal hives, squirming translucent larvae, 

white paint shreds, fragments of gypsum drywall, and strands of pink  

fiberglass insulation, all cascaded onto the floor. Clifton emptied the spray can 

directly onto the carnage until wasps stopped moving, and then, lifting 

the plastic sheet by its corners, he carried the entire bundle outdoors  

to the trash barrel. 

Years later, he described the incident to a pest control technician who  

came to the house (different vermin, rats), and the man said, that was termed 

“chew through” in the trade, not as uncommon as one would think, 

wasps or hornets getting into walls or ceilings and masticating the building’s 

materials to make their nests. In point of fact, the man himself had witnessed 

the aftermath of wasps chewing through the bedroom wall of an elderly 

couple, who were awoken by being stung repeatedly under their bedcovers 

in their nightclothes, and struggled mightily and futilely to escape their predicament, 

neither of them being particularly agile. The tech said these things happen. 

Ever since popping that large pimple in the otherwise solid-seeming  

ceiling, and exposing an infestation of twitching insects beneath 

the paper-thin skin, Clifton was haunted by the feeling that behind every 

surface he thought of as substantial and safe, lurks a hidden chasm full of 

monsters. Not every day, but occasionally, when he walked along the street, 

preoccupied in thought, he saw in the corner of his eye, a blister swelling  

on the side of a building, or on the sidewalk in front of him, or on the back   

of a passing bus, and he wondered what kind of creatures awaited release 

 
 
from within. These days, after a part of his brain died, he has that feeling 

everywhere all the time, sitting there on the couch, as if the hardwood 

floor arrayed before him was made out of painted poster-board, the leather 

hassock at his feet was molded from paper-mache, and the room around him 

was a flimsy cardboard stage flat. He expects at any moment the veneer 

will bow and buckle and reveal the necrotic maggot-riddled ghostly flesh 

behind it, but he has come to believe what he imagines is only the external  

projection of the skin above his left ear concealing the void beneath his skull. 

When they first inherited this house from his wife’s parents, 

before they decided to remodel, Clifton went down in the crawlspace 

to investigate the cause of the warped and sagging floor in the living room. 

Crawling in the dirt on his belly, ducking under the waste pipes and furnace 

return ducts hung from the cobwebbed joists overhead, lit by the crazy shadows  

thrown from the flashlight in his hand, he inched to what he thought 

was the center of the room, a much longer distance it seemed walking above. 

Up ahead, the lengthwise joists rested on a heavy crosswise beam, 

at least four by eight inches, which was in turn supported by short vertical 

4x4 posts resting on concrete pillow blocks, spaced six or seven 

feet apart on the dirt floor. Closer in, Clifton thought sure the places 

where the joists came in contact with the beam were indented and bulging  

a little bit, and that the beam itself sagged slightly in between the posts, 

but couldn’t tell if this was an artifact from his position lying on his stomach 

and craning his neck upwards, or a distortion from the irregular cone 

of light through the lens of his flashlight. Closer yet, Clifton rolled part way 

onto his side and reached out his hand to touch the face of the beam, 

it felt oddly soft and insubstantial, like one of those fake foam beams 

that people used to glue on their dining room ceilings in order to make 

the interior look more antique. He opened the blade on his pocketknife 

 
and stuck it an inch into the wood with very little effort. When the blade 

came back out, it brought out a tiny pyramid of wood, as narrow as the blade, 

along with it. Clifton probed the underside and the face of the beam with the 

knife several more times, with the same result, meeting no more resistance 

than a cereal box of rice cripsies. Dry rot. An invisible fungus which thrives 

in cool dark places and digests the parts of wood which gives it strength 

and stiffness, leaving only the weak porous fibers behind, like transforming 

a beam milled from a tree into a fake beam made out of painted stryofoam. 

How the entire world, trees, rocks, forests, houses, cities, felt to Clifton 

after the stroke, what once was  strong hollowed out from within, invisibly 

eviscerated, just like the section of his brain that once gave him strength. Only  

remedy was to tear out the whole rotten thing and replace it. The beam, that is. 

What he should have done to his 12-year old used Mercedes back in Ohio, 

instead trying to repair the body. Clifton had bought the car used, with 137,000 

miles, but those diesels last for at least three times that, white sedan, like-new 

red interior, unfortunately Midwestern winters are hell on unibody construction, 

with all the road salt. The underside was undercoated, but the rocker panels 

below the doors were paint finish, and blemished with spots of reddish-brown 

rust, like the car was a gleaming white molar with plaque along the gum line, 

or a great white whale beset with barnacles on its belly. Lying on his side 

on the garage floor, Clifton ground down all the rusty blotches, plugged any 

holes with epoxy resin and fiberglass cloth, filled the contours 

with plastic body putty, filed and sanded the curves smooth, primed and 

wet sanded again, sprayed three coats of factory white alkyd enamel, 

and rubbed out with polishing compound, and waxed, the right and left lower sides,  

wheel-well to wheel-well, of the Mercedes. Looked almost showroom new,  

for six months. Then pinhole-sized dark spots gradually appeared, grew to 

nickel-sized in a couple months, and within a year the rocker panels 

 
rusted out as if Clifton had never spent weeks on the floor doing the repair.  

The metal corroded from the inside out, it didn’t matter how much work was 

done on the surface or how good the surface looked when the work was done, 

the hidden moisture and salt trapped in the internal cavities would 

eventually eat the steel from the inside. Clifton kept the car for years, 

put another 100,000 miles on the odometer, but he stopped washing and waxing 

it anymore, it had become another old beater. He sold it just about the time 

his first marriage fell apart. 

The marriage lasted six years, or four more years than that  

counting the on-again off-again dating relationship, two years less to  

the first signs of inexorable decay breaking through to the surface. Beginning 

in the college library, Janie sat down at Clifton’s table, something about 

them being in the same survey section, she’s pretty cute, do you study 

all the time, over coffee he’s charming and funny, does she actually like him, 

in her apartment they talk the stars to bed, spend most nights together 

as one body, summer apart, back at school move in together, in the spring 

they think they should see other people, serious let-down jobs after graduation, 

at bars and weekends the others last through winter, then dinner with each other 

for old time’s sake ends up in bed, they reconcile as used-to-each-other best 

friends, realistically facing the future might-as-well marry, the usual changes 

of addresses and employers and acquaintances and automobiles and pets 

lasts a decade. After which Clifton saw the light fade from Janie’s face like  

from a years-long dimmer switch, imperceptible day-to-day, noticeable only 

in as how other people’s children have grown up. Nothing apparently missing, 

nothing obviously depleted, a glass neither half empty nor half full, 

rather the water transformed into a weaker flimsier version of water. 

That’s how he sensed, or how he didn’t sense, Janie’s love. He never had been 

a religious person, but he read about the experience of the withdrawal 

 
of god’s presence from the world in the modern era, and he imagined 

he felt the same way, from Janie’s growing unpresence in their marriage. 

A gap in the rainbow, not a hole, a suspicion there’s a color he can’t see 

anymore that used to illumine his life. A hollowing out, a hidden blind spot, 

a reduction in space to a fraction less than three dimensions, a diminution   

in time that stretches out the thin seconds, an erosion of what he imagined 

he once was in her eyes. As if Janie ripped apart the photographs of her memories 

and threw out all the parts containing his image, and the torn scraps of him  

ceased to exist. He had the same feeling years later, after he long since 

adjusted to living in a world no longer lit by her love, and pretended not 

to know the difference, when the whole world gave up the ghost of meaning 

in the aftermath of his stroke, but permanently, unalterably, as if his divorce 

from Janie was a knife cut, the blade in deep, and this was an amputation,  

which in point of fact it was, the hacking off a piece of brain. It could be  

the brain functions like a hologram, as some scientists theorize. Memories  

and experiences and consciousness and all mental phenomena are distributed 

over regions of the cortex, much like the light from an object is captured 

for every point in the hologram, and a version of the entire scene 

can be regenerated from any portion of the hologram, at reduced resolution.  

Unlike tearing a photograph in pieces, in which what remains is a piece 

the original image, what remains in the partially dead holographic brain 

is diminished version of the former self, in which reality blisters, 

paper thin, as exoskeleton molts and snake skin sheds, or softens and crumbles,  

as the connective tissue dissolves, or corrodes from the inside, as pits and cavities  

eat away at the skin, or the scent of love dissipates from a broken window.
