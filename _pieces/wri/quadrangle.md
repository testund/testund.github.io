Foster stands outside the prison gates inside the living room. 

Across the road, the flat wasteland stretches to the low mountains. 

The overcast sky tarnishes to gunmetal, and a slice of green fluorescence 

from the door unclosed to the hallway cuts the blanket at his waist. 

He feels the chain-link fence at his back, and, at twice his height, 

the intertwined spirals of razor wire, glistening blue under the floodlights 

from the recessed soffit cans. Stars cast unseen shadows on clouds, 

pinhole perforations in acoustic tile, indents in skip-troweled drywall, 

and the undulating blobs of darkness swimming under his eyelids. 

The door opens wider and bleaches the bed down to the footboard.    

Foster’s dream dissolves into a white flare filtered through his eyelashes, 

and reconstructs the story of the hospital and the episode and before. 

The night nurse bends over him and performs a perfunctory well check. 

He focuses on the wall clock as its face fades in a narrowing quadrangle 

when she closes the door behind her to a width of an eye.  

The sheets twist around his front-to-back doubled gowns as he tries 

to shoulder himself higher into the pillow on the half-elevated mattress. 

One side’s elbow and hip and heel dislodge the other side of his body, 

and manage to lever his head upward a chin-width. 

The tubing tightens against the tape on his thigh, and he pulls it out 

from under the bottom of the bedrail enough to make a half loop. 

His belly rises and falls slowly beneath the covers as he settles  

into the long journey to rejoin the dream, or enter another. 

Faint gray arcs trace the tracks of tires turning both ways 

from the gravel driveway, smaller radii and less dense toward the right, 

big quarter circles attenuating almost across the road on the left. 

Like brush strokes dipped in wet blue-gray stone, curving the bristles dry. 

Foster follows a counterclockwise path to the far edge of the asphalt, 

 
 
voting with the majority, and proceeds down the road with his back 

to traffic, if there would be any. His left foot lingers long on the pavement 

as his right leg comes around, and he pitches starboard to make room for it. 

After a quarter mile, the lights that cast his shadow before him gradually expire 

into the diffuse gloom.  The dusty black sand on the roadside contrasts 

to the deeper black of the road surface itself, fading out to the grayer black 

in the distance, ending darker in the purple black of the low mountains, 

offset by the faintly greenish black of the lowering sky.  He can’t see 

boundaries between road and ground and wilderness and foothills and clouds 

except peripherally, and his half-closed and out of focus eyes deny him 

any way to measure his forward progress, like crawling underwater or 

sleepwalking through a sandstorm. The air respirates through his mouth 

as empty as a vacuum. The only sound comes from his footfalls, the gravel  

percussive crunch of his left shoe, alternating with the sandy snare slide 

of the right, One-two, up tempo dirge. When had he left the pavement? 

Maybe the road turned and he missed it, maybe the paving ended 

and turned to dirt, maybe he got turned around. No point in turning back,  

nothing to return to, might as well keep going.  

Two paces, pivot on the weaker right, swing the stronger left, 

torso turning half around, straighten the right in a half-step, stride left, 

two paces back to the door, repeat same left-leading clockwise turn, 

two paces alongside the cantilevered cot to the metal sink and toilet, turn, 

repeat. The gray-green of the concrete walls darkens a shade to green-gray 

when the scratched plastic of the book-sized window in the upper door 

momentarily fills with a guard’s head, eclipsing the hallway fixtures 

and attenuating the residual lights-out level from the cell’s ceiling panels. 

They don’t like Foster to pace at night, but how else he can tire himself 

enough to fall asleep, since in his condition he can’t do calisthenics, 

 
and they only let him out in the quadrangle for a half hour a day. Less when 

they write up him for a infraction, such pacing after lights-out. 

But this guard, he calls him Jug-ears, obvious given the size of his ears, 

guards wear only numbers not name badges on their uniforms, wouldn’t do that. 

Other guards prefer to subdue him in his cell to stop him from pacing all night, 

spray or tase or rough him up, instead delaying punishment of limited time 

outside until another shift, which he prefers anyway, because the aftereffects 

of the trauma make him feel alive, and tired enough to sleep. Tonight, 

after walking for three hours, although he can’t a see clock, but he has had  

a long time to learn how to count his traversals across the nine foot span,  

he feels like a smoothly oscillating piston with no need for rest. 

Pulling the afghan to one side, Foster sits heavily on the center cushion 

of the sofa. He grabs his right knee and swings both legs up, while lowering 

his head onto the throw pillow against the opposite armrest.  The bedroom 

doors of the house stand closed, he doesn’t like going where his family used 

to sleep, last time he entered the bedrooms to block the registers with cardboard  

to save heat, he didn’t linger or look around. Since the incident, he’s taken 

to sleeping on the couch in the living area, or not sleeping, laying there 

awake trying not to think about anything. He covers himself with the afghan, 

and inclines his eyes toward the television in the cabinet against the wall. 

The convex screen darkly reflects the room ballooned up, bloated, fish-eyed, 

the walls curving to meet the ceiling, the couch off-center, small, 

and bent like a brown banana, a tiny face lost in a crook of an arm.  

The television doesn’t work anymore, or it works, but stations have all 

changed their broadcasting formats. After buying and hooking up 

a converter box and a new antennae in what he imagined was 

the correct fashion, the only reason he could think for the failure 

to receive the new programming, was that the antennae should have been 

 
mounted on the roof. Because he certainly could not do that himself, 

and hiring someone else do it was clearly impossible, he watched 

the old stations, one by one, leave the air, and turn to white noise. 

No matter, he had only tuned in the news anyway, out of habit. 

The graying edge-gaps between the window blinds and the wall 

make the black rectangle of the television recede from the grooved paneling 

which holds the mounting bracket, and the noises from out in the hallway, 

indistinct voices, intermittent laughter, metal carts colliding, a chirping 

errant wheel, a neglected monitor beeping, alarm another day.  

Noises less disturbing than sharing the room with another patient  

who keeps the television on dawn till dark of night, blasting talk shows, 

and the dull green privacy curtain, grommet-hooked from its channel which 

bridges the off-white tracks of the suspended tile grid, screens the picture,  

but not the sound. Watching the room lighten, now fully awake, Foster 

wants a cup of coffee, but he knows not to press the call button to  

summon an orderly, because the staff doesn’t like to be bothered, especially 

at the end of their shift, or at the beginning of another one, and waiting 

for a half an hour for a tepid, weak, acidic styrofoam cup of brown water  

isn’t worth enhancing his reputation as trouble-maker. Lying on his back 

on the thin mattress that covers his cot, more of a steel shelf sticking out 

of the wall than an actual bed, he keeps his eyes closed, though 

he still hasn’t slept, so as not to antagonize any of the first-shift guards, 

who tend to be harder-core than the evening or night shift, because 

they’re more closely supervised. The squeak of the one stuck wheel 

on the meal cart alternates with the clangs of the slots in the doors opening 

and clamor of trays slamming down, as breakfast, such as it is, advances  

up the hallway.  They are waiting for him. One ahead of him, on the upper 

level of the walkway, but when he turns to descend the stairs, two more 

 
come up behind him. The three grab him by the arms and punch him 

and throw him to the floor and kick him and pick up him and push him 

headfirst over the railing. A bolted-down table-bench unit on the lower level 

partially breaks his fall. His skull above the left ear dents the edge 

of the table, and his body cartwheels over the bench to the concrete floor, 

right arm and ankle broken. He pulls the blanket away and rolls on his side, 

knees bent, pressing his palm on the edge of the cot, slowly curling 

his body into a sitting position, and swinging his legs in front of him. 

His feet find his slippers, no shoelaces here, no matter, he can’t tie 

them one handed anyway. 

The ground feels uncertain, uneven, composed of looser, larger, 

stones mixed in with the sandy dirt than before, so he has to slow  

his already slow pace, and carefully place his steps, because if he falls, 

he would be unable to get up without something at least knee-high to lean on. 

Up ahead, a blocky shape materializes gray out of the darker featureless 

desolation of the plain. Closer, it resolves into a waist-high rock outcropping, 

flat on top, a ledge crossing the near face at a angle, like a incomplete ziggurat, 

or stair-stepped altar. Foster eases down to sit two-thirds along the incline, 

his legs abruptly exhausted and trembling after he halts his forward progress. 

After a few minutes, his back aches from the strain of resting on the narrow shelf, 

so he raises himself up and lifts his legs to lie down on the mostly flat, 

slightly convex, upper surface of the massive slab. Hands crossed 

over his chest, eyes closed, surprisingly comfortable, he relaxes. 

Maybe they transferred a new prisoner into his former cell by now, 

obliterating any memory of his time. Oncoming headlights cross 

into his lane and startle him blindingly awake. His daughter screams 

“Daddy!” right before impact. Pebbles dig into his back and shoulders 

as he shifts positions on the rock. Pivoting on one elbow, he slides 

off the sofa onto his feet, but his legs buckle beneath him,  

and he ends up sideways sitting on his right hip on the carpet, as far 

as he can tell, unhurt. The frame of the tray table blocks his view of the door, 

so he pushes it away, tracing an arc on the linoleum from its casters. 

“Mister Foster, what do you think you’re doing?” The orderly 

enters the room quickly but not urgently, and stands over Foster. 

“You know you can’t get of bed by yourself.” Foster recognizes him 

as the one he calls Tattoo, a bulky shaved-skull young man 

bearing what might be faded gang insignia. The orderly bends down, 

grips his hands under Foster’s armpits, and lifts him to his feet, 

light and fragile as a fledgling baby bird fallen from its nest. 

“There you go. No harm, no foul.” Tattoo lies him down on the bed, 

checks his catheter, and recovers him with the bedding. “You don’t 

want to do that again, or else we’ll have to restrain you. You wouldn’t 

like that.” Softening his voice, he turns to the door. “Now, you need 

anything else?” 

“Coffee.” Foster croaks. 

“Your breakfast will be here in a couple minutes.” 

Foster presses the button on the inside panel of the upper bedrail, 

and the top half of the bed frame sandwiches the mattress, and thereby folds 

his head and shoulders into a half-sitting position. With his left hand, 

he places his inert right arm halfway across his belly, closes his eyes, 

and settles back in the pillow. The heavy, swamp-like smell of breakfast 

being unearthed seeps from the cafeteria carts in the hallway. He refills his glass 

from the nearly empty bottle of cheap Sangoivese on the counter, takes 

a healthy mouthful, overripe with a hint of wet leaves, and tops it off before 

returning to the couch. Plenty of time to watch the ten o’clock news 

before he needs to drive over to the kids’ high school dance for the pick-up. 

 
 
 
 
He relaxes into the familiar evening lethargy, limbs limp as a warm bath, 

face tingling like coming in from the snow, scalp lifting from the skull, 

thinking mercifully about nothing that he can remember, eyes closing 

for an instant. The steering wheel turns light and cool in his grip, 

as he tries to avoid the oncoming headlights, but he only succeeds 

in deflecting the force of the collision to the passenger-side front fender, 

where it peels open much of that side of his car, before sending 

him sideways across traffic into the opposite guardrail, which halts 

the momentum of the left side of his skull against, and through, 

the window. The steam rising from the crumpled hood softens 

the illumination of the roadside trees from the one working headlight 

pointing upward at a crazy angle. He lifts his head from the door frame 

enough to croak, “Annie?” 

“Better pull yourself together and eat your breakfast, Foster.” 

The guard clangs the tray on the shelf welded to the inside of the door slot. 

A faint odor of burnt grease wafts from the brown serving of nutraloaf, 

a mashed up mixture of bread beans vegetables and occasionally meat, 

formed into blocks and baked, no utensils required, for those who eat only 

to keep on living.  Foster bites off a corner, chews it without thinking, 

swallows. Remarkable what a person can get used to, crippled, confined. 

Back when had the means to kill himself, he lacked the motivation, 

or the courage. Settled for drinking enough every night to submerge into sleep. 

Now, when he’s nearly given up hope, he lacks the means. Other than 

refusing to eat, but they would force a feeding tube down his throat anyway.  

His mouth feels like a dusty cuff of his dungarees, his tongue can’t 

milk enough spit out of his cheeks to swallow. His feet plod onward, 

a mindless mechanism. His right shoulder aches, holding up the arm so long, 

and he clasps his hands together. The terrain slopes uphill, the ground flatter, 

 
less rocky, underfoot. Maybe the daytime light would soon be switching on 

in his cell, the routine of the day starting again, no decisions, at least 

he could take a drink from the sink. The coffin dome overhead leaks a bit 

where it doesn’t fit the ragged hills ringing the horizon. A hulking form 

detaches from the underside of the range, darker against the bluish gray 

of the plain off in the distance, and looming now saddle-shaped against 

the greenish gray of the sky. Coming closer, the shape resolves 

to a brownish black crumpled mass of interconnected oblongs, 

like monstrous horseshoe crab shell left behind when seas receded, 

an abandoned and burned-out panel truck, wheel-less and windowless, 

with its roof pounded into a serving bowl. Maybe he is still on the road, 

or the truck was as lost as he is when it broke down. He skirts to its rear,  

where one back door hangs open and the other lays on the ground.  

The gurney folds its wheels into the back of the ambulance, 

jarring him back into the present, except for the right half 

of his body, which remains lifeless, numb, or maybe asleep. 

The paramedic leans over him. “How many fingers…your name… 

know where you…year?...ever happen to you before? Any pain? 

You apparently had a cerebrovascular accident. A stroke. Don’t worry, 

we’ll have you at the hospital in ten minutes.” 

“No.” Foster whispers. They must have taken Annie in another 

ambulance. Would they have called her mother? Couldn’t have been 

that serious an accident, don’t see any blood, it doesn’t hurt.  

What happened to the car? They didn’t ask me if I was drinking, 

it wasn’t any more than usual. That guy came over into my lane, 

nothing I could do.  He sits up on the rug, leaning one elbow 

on the couch cushion, bending one leg beneath him, lifting the other 

onto one knee. Breathing heavily, he corkscrews to his feet, coming up 

 
in a half squat, before unsteadily straightening all the way up. 

The room comes into focus, the early morning light graying the blinds, 

overwhelming the parchment lampshade left on all night on the end table. 

He hobbles to the kitchen sink, rinses a glass, fills it again, drinks it down, 

starts the coffee. Opening the cupboard, he takes a nutribar out of the box,  

meal replacement, oatmeal raisin. Since the microwave died in a shower of sparks, 

and the refrigerator stopped keeping things cold, he has simplified his diet.   

Which mostly eliminates his trips to the store, and thereby he avoids  

driving the car, because although the power steering turns one-handed, 

the gas and brake pedals can’t differentiate between the amount of pressure 

applied by his insensitive right foot, which makes him fear not for his life, 

that, he would be relieved to give up, but for getting into a situation, 

such as an accident, that would lead to giving up control of his life again 

to other people. He carries the cup of coffee back to the sofa, sets it down 

on the end table before he sits on that side. The tray table overhangs 

the blanket over his belly, and offers a brown plastic tray, still wet from  

the dishwasher, bearing juice, coffee, yogurt, a banana about to turn brown, 

and a bowl of beige, congealed, glistening what seems to be oatmeal. 

He sips the coffee. 

“All done with your breakfast?” The orderly, must be new, 

skinny, bad skin, buck teeth, Foster decides to name him Bugs, lifts  

the tray with one arm while the other arm spins the tray table aside.  

The slot in the door opens and the metal tray disappears with a dull clank. 

The metallic nutribar wrapper slowly uncrumples on end table, 

its curling reflections from the lamp running like pooled mercury. 

Foster’s teeth ache, his head hurts, his eyes itch, but his body 

limps onward like a mindless automaton. Blinking away the spots 

that obscure his vision, he thinks he sees a faint brightness near 

 
the horizon, so he turns his head to one side, and looks again. 

Definitely something out there. After a few more minutes walking, 

the brightness coalesces into a vague horizontal band. A few minutes 

more, the shape separates into a row of bright blobs, and eventually, 

bringing him to a full stop, forming distinct blue-white mercury vapor 

floodlights shining on a low gray strip, a façade, a wall, an illuminated 

fortress. The prison. Could he have wandered in a circle, and ended up 

right back where he started from? He kneels on one knee, and from there, 

falls heavily backward, on his backside, right in the dirt, bending 

his legs out in front of him, grasping his right knee in his left hand, 

knowing full well it’s likely he can’t get up again.  He closes his eyes 

and rests his head on the back of the couch. Maybe he can doze off 

again, too much time to fill, all day alone in the house. Maybe he could 

try to write again, something to occupy his mind, fiction, poetry, anything.  

He slumps lower in the bed. Soon somebody will come to help him dress  

and brush his teeth. Then the transfer to his wheelchair to go to morning 

therapy. After that lunch. Maybe he can nap after lunch. He leans 

back against the rolled-up mattress on the steel cot and wraps 

the gray blanket across his chest, waiting for the guards to come 

to take him out in the quadrangle for a half hour of sky.
