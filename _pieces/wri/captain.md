Walking a step behind my wife on the downtown sidewalk, 

a little like swaggering, one hip hiking like a pirate’s peg leg, 

starboard weight-bearing knee swashbuckling, rolling on my sea legs,  

wooden foot slapping flat on the scored concrete plank, 

one hand coming to an abrupt end in a curved claw  

clamped on my waistcoat like a spring-loaded vise-grip pliers, 

yellow-headed service parrot pincering my shoulder with its talons, 

transposing my guttural arrr-praxia oaths into high-pitched clichés, 

I masquerade as Hook. 

Little yappy dogs on long leashes, several lost boys on skateboards, 

double-wide jogging strollers, snickering tinkerbells four abreast, 

joystick power wheelchair, not yet lovelorn couple rapt arm hip-draped, 

and, in the commuter lane, praying to his ear, a venture capitalist, 

pitch and yaw me enough to stop short, bracingly becalmed, 

bottoming out eye contact on a mortal speed bump. 

No empathy reflects from the glass plate of the Stone Cold Creamery, 

rather a frozen dissolve halfway between streetscape: opposite bank facade, 

beached glistening black SUV, terrazzo planter garnished with boxwood, 

insect-eyed ponytailed passerby with handheld Cheeky Monkey bag, me, 

and, interior tableau: fairly bearded caregiver bent at his device 

sideways to a rippled glass-top wrought-iron café table facing a Giants 

bowed orange-black cap and warm-up jacket, its elbows on the padded arms 

of a wheelchair, thick fingers encircling the white cardboard container from which 

a mirror hand plastic-spoons chocolate ice cream to a conjectured mouth. 

I lost my taste for dessert before it discovered the sweet oasis of the afternoon. 

More often than not, on my mid-day way to or from town, 

 
I pass the teenage boy who lives nearby, sucking on his fingers, 

slouched in his wheelchair, pushed by either one of two slight Asian women 

who serve as his alternate caretakers, along the seams of sidewalk or 

the side of the pavement, for the section of the street that has no sidewalks, 

the three-quarters of a mile to or from the corner of major through-street’s 

golden arches, where presumably he enjoys his daily happy meal. 

My youngest son attended the elementary school that mainstreamed this boy. 

One day in outpatient neuro rehab, I shared a session with a boy, 

now young man, who was my eldest son’s classmate through fifth grade, 

on side by side treadmills, his straddled by a tubular frame which, via 

straps and pulleys and winches, held him suspended from a harness like 

the ones that parachutists wear, so his sneakers barely grazed the rubber. 

Braces besieged his right foot and hand, scars assaulted the left side of his skull.  

Near fatal victim of a head-on collision where his fellow band-mates in the van 

died, his mother explained, waiting behind his wheelchair for the elevator. 

At least I’ve had three score years with two good arms to hold my children, 

two good legs to play tag with them, while my son’s childhood playmate 

merely played his opening club gigs before he never knew what hit his brain. 

Another day, when I was awaiting my physical therapy appointment, 

a man limped to the registration desk, about my age, skinny, tall, 

worn jeans, flannel shirt, fishing cap pulled over collar-length graying hair, 

leaned his cane against the edge of the counter, reached for his back pocket, 

and, with a fluid and practiced flip of his hand, extracted his insurance card 

from his wallet, presented it between thumb and forefinger to the receptionist. 

His left arm pendulum’d from his side as he turned, palm facing rearward, 

fingers curved inward, left foot pivoted from the knee and pointing outward, 

the signs of a right hemisphere stroke, which enviably spared his speech, 

his dominant hand, his gas pedal foot. He stood there alone, he drives, 

or walks to the bus by himself, maybe he lives alone, he has gotten used to, 

adapted to, this rest of his life, hard to imagine me doing any of those. 

We look enough alike to be half brothers, perhaps we should merge  

the unimpacted halves of our brains and form one undamaged person. 

Or, ala Jekyll and Hyde, somehow mirror the hemispheres of my brain, 

and create two people, one quadriplegic, the other with all limbs able. 

Of course, the fleet-footed version of me couldn’t speak, in fact, 

he lacks language altogether, because language skill dwells 

in the left half of the brain, copied in his case from the uncorrupted right.   

The paralyzed me, on the other side, talks marginally, like I do now, 

and comprehends language better, having twice the mental capacity, 

reading, listening, memorizing, and composing, but unfortunately 

cannot write at all. Dictation is possible, given a scribe or machine 

patient and savvy enough to translate my halting and garbled words. 

The interesting question is how to choose between: being fully in the body, 

like a deer leaping in the forest, with no words to conceive the sensation, 

the words that differentiate humans from animals, or: a sedentary or supine 

thinking being, in dialogue with other minds past and present, self-aware, 

sapient as a homo sapiens. Dumb beast or disembodied poet. 

Feral boy or clever cripple.  Agile dancer or bed-bound philosopher. 

Tai Chi master waves hands like clouds, while consciousness  

uploads into the Cloud, our inert body left behind when we levitate 

our texting mind beyond the rapturous sheen of the screen. 

Fortunately or unfortunately, I don’t have to make the choice, because 

in this screenplay, I’m cast in the role of Captain Hook, neither whole  

in body nor mind, after a crocodile has bitten a good chunk out of my brain, 

and I hear the ticking of the clock counting down until he returns to finish, 

while I dream wordlessly of the land where I never have grow up.
