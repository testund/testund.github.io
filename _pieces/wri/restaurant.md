The chair was remarkably uncomfortable, even for a restaurant chair, 

whose purpose is to discourage lingering after dessert, Sid thought. Green 

leather, well, vinyl, covered plywood seat, no give there. Thick rectangular 

wooden spindles on the back, arranged in a concave vertical array, sort of 

like a coarse crosswise comb, pinching the spine and shoulder blades no 

matter how the torso rotates. The nearly right angle between the seat and the 

back precludes slouching for more than a few minutes because the lumbar 

region begins to ache, and the chair’s legs are so short, if the shoes soles are 

flat on the floor, the thighs are lifted from the seat, transferring all the more 

weight to the tailbone. The alternative, knees bent and feet up on the toes 

between the front legs beneath the seat, forces the posture upright and drives 

the spine further into the back slats. The designers of this chair must have 

never sat in it, or maybe the company they worked for required them to stand 

at their drafting tables, or, even worse, made them sit in the chairs they 

designed, and this was their revenge on everybody else who had to endure 

their creation. Plus, the legs wobble, lousy glue, probably manufactured in 

Asia, as most furniture is these days, some kind of monkey wood, tropical, 

close grain, lightweight, stained to look reddish brown like mahogany, 

medium gloss, though the thick varnish shows scuffs and scratches of heavy 

use. Case in point, this attempt to turn the chair partially sideways to the table 

by screeching the feet across the floor tile, catching at the grout seams, and 

lurching up and sidewise, with the table edge grinding against the side of the 

stile just below the top rail. 

“Are you okay? Can I help you?” Nancy asked from across table, 

interrupting the kids’ conversation. 

“No. Fine. Just trying.” Sid thought about saying, trying to find a more 

comfortable position in this lousy chair, but there was no point in taking the 

time and effort that it would take to explain this, besides there was nothing 

 
 
anybody could do about it, except get up and leave, and he didn’t want to be 

a whiner. His wife Nancy paid attention to his slightest sigh, mutter, or 

twitch, always attuned to the merest hint of negativity. She said that she was 

only looking out for his best possible recovery, but he felt like he was serving 

a life sentence in the prison of positive thinking, in which any violation of the 

rule against complaining merited the punishment of being left by himself. 

The conversation resumed, his daughter Ariana was talking, but she had the 

habit of speaking so rapidly, as most members of her demographic do these 

days, due perhaps to the speed of modern electronic communications among 

the young, that Sid could not understand her, especially in the noisy 

restaurant. 

“…landlord…drunk…garbage…Melissa…finals…” Ariana seemed to 

say, her usual anxieties about college life. Nancy inclined her head toward 

her daughter with a sympathetic half smile. Aaron, seated beside his father, 

sipped his sake and nonchalantly checked out the other patrons and 

waitpersons, lingering over the younger females. Sid lamented both those 

vices, the latter because he had never been as comfortable in all things sexual 

as his son, even at his son’s age, although he and Aaron never talked about 

such things. Better his son than him at this point, he was all used up, while 

Aaron was just getting started. Regarding the former, Sid considered whether 

he should order a beer, but it was not worth risking Nancy’s silently deferred 

disapproval, when what he wanted was not one bottle of beer, rather two 

entire bottles of budget Sangiovese, or a half dozen double gin and diet 

tonics, enough alcohol to make his mind as comfortably numb as his right 

hand and his lower arm, since the stroke. With his left hand, he reached out 

and rotated the water glass on the laminate tabletop, blurring the ring of 

condensation into an oval, and then lifting the glass slightly several times, to 

trace multiple concentric circles, in an approximation of a spirograh, or a 

 
Ballantine’s logo. He considered raising the glass to his mouth and taking a 

sip of water, but he wasn’t thirsty, just looking for something to do, and one 

sip would lead to another, might as well stop before he started. Furthermore, 

he learned to limit his liquid intake in the evenings, otherwise he’d be up 

every two hours at night. The hand returned his lap as he became aware that 

Nancy was talking to him. 

“…about the parrot.” His wife smiled at him, trying him draw into the 

conversation. 

“No. Can’t hear. What?” Sid said. 

“You remember. What was his name? Mikey? The one you inherited 

from the old lady with the farm,” Nancy said. His son and daughter turned to 

him, not out of anticipation, they had heard all his stories before, more out of 

a mixture of affection and obligation. Sid didn’t want to try to tell the story, 

but neither did he want to refuse outright his family, well, his wife, the kids 

wouldn’t care. He gave it a shot. 

“Um. Basically. We dar…” That wasn’t the right sound. Hard C, like 

K, as in car. “Car…” Long A, like air. “Um. We care take…” What was the 

vowel in the past tense. “Toke…car toke” Give up. Start over. “Basically. 

Back when we lived on the farm.” Who was we. Sid’s ex-wife, the anxiety 

about attempting to pronounce her name. Skip it, keep going. “Um. The 

parrot was…” The old lady’s name. “Jy…” Tongue not touching the roof of 

the mouth. “No. Parrot belonged to Chy…” That wasn’t it. Voiced G. No, too 

much trouble to explain. “Um. Basically. The parrot’s name was Kikey.” Sid 

stopped. 

Aaron came to his rescue. “Because the old lady said, look at his nose. 

But after you got it you changed his name to Mikey.” 

 
 
 
 
 
“Yes. Pretty much,” Sid said. Nancy nodded, pleased to have her 

husband and her children talking together, such as it was. They all went silent 

for a moment, then Ariana continued, addressing her brother. 

“Do you think … tell Sam … Katy said …” Someone or other, Sid lost 

track. When would the waiter come back to take their order, people at other 

tables who came in after them were already being served their entrees, the 

place was getting really crowded, the entry area beyond the cash register was 

full of people waiting to be seated, or maybe carry-out customers waiting 

their orders, in either case, the kitchen would soon be swamped, if it wasn’t 

already. Not that Sid was particularly hungry, these days he ate the same rote 

meals at the same times every day just to keep his body living, without much 

enthusiasm for the food, or the living. Since the stroke, he’d lost his appetite, 

not just for food. For sex, because the limp arm and game leg left him less 

than half a man. For conservation, because nobody would wait willingly for 

him finish a sentence. For literature, what he once loved as much as life, 

because the words on the page, whether reading other’s or writing himself, 

had gone silent, the inner music lost its meaning. For his family and 

colleagues, because he lost interest in all their comings and goings and 

busyness when he was reduced to mute idleness. The idea of going out to a 

restaurant was Nancy’s, because the kids came back home on winter break 

from college. Aaron and Ariana. They were strangers to him, adults, with 

their own anxieties and failures, the promise of who they could grow up to be 

was betrayed. Well, nothing Sid could do about that now. And Nancy. Hard 

to imagine how he once lusted for her, his confidante, his muse, his first 

reader. Reduced to his nursemaid, his caretaker. All of them would be better 

without him, at least in this restaurant, Sid was content to have dinner at 

home. Although the meal out did break up the day, not only getting ready to 

go and driving over and back and the duration of the meal itself, by the looks 

 
 
of it, stretching out to maybe over two hours, if that waiter didn’t make his 

appearance soon, but also the way that having something to do altered the 

experience of time for the entire day, changing it from a gray undifferentiated 

slough to a warm ramping anticipation, even though what was looked 

forward to was not particularly enjoyable. Nancy was saying something. 

“…order for you, Sid?”   

“Um. No. What?” Sid noticed the waiter standing at the end of the 

table beside Aaron, order pad and pen at the ready. 

“Do you want me to order for you, or do you want to do it yourself?” 

repeated Nancy. 

“No. Do it yourself.” Sid removed his reading glasses from his shirt 

pocket, shook the temple frames free, and placed the bridge on his nose. The 

right earpiece missed looping his ear, his left hand crossed his face to hook it 

over the upper helix, and adjusted the lenses downward on the way back 

across. Leaning over the menu on the tabletop, he tipped his head back to 

bring the fine print into focus. He certainly knew what he wanted, they had 

been coming to this Japanese place for years, not too much to eat, not too 

expensive, bite sized pieces, so nobody has to cut up his meal like child. 

“Um. Actually. I have a danebo wore.” 

“Benbo box?” The waiter nodded. 

“Yes. Um. NO. Wapo wool!” Sid read down through the specialty 

sushi rolls searching for another one he would both like, and able to 

pronounce. 

The waiter looked dismayed. “Please, yellowtail?” 

Picking up the menu with one hand, Sid attempted to indicate his 

selection with his thumb, but unfortunately, when the waiter peered past 

Aaron, who leaned back in his chair out of the way, the tip of the thumb 

covered the desired item.  

 
 
 
 
“I think he means the rainbow roll,” Aaron said. 

The waiter leaned back and wrote in his pad, Aaron righted himself, 

and Sid attempted to lower his menu, still wearing his reading glasses, which 

defocused the target landscape, so on the way down, the side of the menu 

caught the throat of his full water glass. While the glass didn’t tip over, it 

wobbled, sending water sloshing over the rim. Nancy was the first to react, 

flinging her napkin up from her lap to cover the creeping shoreline of the 

small sea, more of a puddle actually, before its tide reached the edge of the 

table. Ariana flinched sideways in her chair, Aaron held his napkin poised 

aloft, the waiter bent over and with Ariana’s napkin dabbed up the remaining 

spill, and Sid muttered, “Sorry. Sorry.” 

“That’s alright, honey,” said Nancy, and went on to address the waiter 

with her order. Everything was hassle to Sid, even the simple act of ordering 

in a restaurant, the whole process of going out, getting his shoes on, he 

couldn’t tie the laces by himself, getting his coat on, he couldn’t find the arm 

hole behind his back, getting into and out of the car, picking up his knee so 

his shoe would clear the sill and then needing help to close and open the door 

and buckle and unbuckle the seatbelt, lowering himself into the seat and 

standing up again, everything. And now he had knocked over the water. After 

the debacle of trying to pronounce the name of the rolls. They told him he 

was making good progress, look at how far he had come since the hospital. 

Sid certainly remembered escaping from lying flat on his back on the front 

porch of hell, but that didn’t make him particularly thankful to be alive. 

Rather, he looked back at the time before the stroke, at everything he used to 

enjoy, walking in the early morning fog, late afternoons in his study with his 

books, feeling in control of his life, feeling really good sometimes, as some 

kind of lost paradise, not that he was never unhappy, depressed even, then, 

but he remembered being in a state of grace, whole, alive. Not the living 

death, like now. Stuck between life and death, in purgatory, limbo, as if that 

collapse on the floor had killed him and left a pseudo-being, an automaton, in 

his place. Each day a gauntlet to run through, as if he could still run, hobbled, 

the double row of unfilled hours clawing at him, the finish line but a restless 

sleep, broken, waking in order to start all over again. Wife and kids and 

therapy appointments and books and bathing and former acquaintances and 

news and walks and tooth brushing and mail, all just clocks ticks measuring 

what was lost to him. This endless restaurant meal too, counting down the 

steps on the escalator to the void. Sid shifted in the chair, stiffening up, 

sitting too long, wondered if any cultures buried their dead sitting up, he read 

about Buddhist monks mummified sitting in the lotus position, maybe he 

would be mummified in this goddamn chair. Ariana was saying something he 

couldn’t understand.
