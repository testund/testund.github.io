Something was seriously wrong with the squirrel in the middle of 

the backyard. Usually, when digging in the fallen leaves or the grass,  

squirrels let me get about twenty feet away before they jerk their heads  

sideways, freeze, and then rifle for the nearest tree trunk, legs a blur, 

tail streamlined straight out behind. This one, however, alternated between 

twisting its body by contorting its hind legs, and straightening out again, 

with the regularity of a metronome, as if the mulch beneath its feet 

were pulsating with electricity, without making much forward progress. 

I held back from approaching the squirrel, while possible explanations 

for its behavior occurred to me. Maybe it fell from a tree and broke 

its back, maybe it suffered some kind seizure, or parasites, or rat poison, 

or rabies. I considered whether I should kill it, I had seen squirrel guns, 

small caliber pistols with long barrels and large scopes, but I hadn’t one 

of those, or any other guns. Because I didn’t want come too close to it, 

the only possible lethal weapon I could think of was the pointed shovel 

on the other side of the house. No problem reaching out for the squirrel 

with the blade on its six foot handle, open question how to apply 

enough leverage to crush or cut it in half. On the other hand, maybe 

I should’ve called the police, but I could hear myself trying to explain 

why they should dispatch a car because a squirrel was acting, well, 

nutty on my lawn. 

Seeing a squirrel, or for that matter any wild animal, except the occasional 

broken wing bird, in so obvious distress is a rare occurrence where I live. 

 
 
 
I’ve known enough domesticated animals in extremis to suspect that humans  

have no exclusive claim to pain and suffering. My future ex-wife’s 

white German shepherd, Heather, with inbred hip dysplasia, dragging her 

hindquarters, whimpering. Mrs. Evan’s old bay horse, Mitzi, badly foundered, 

hooves curled like elf shoes, open sores piercing the hide on its flank. 

The Hereford cow, I didn’t catch her name, her prolapsed uterus glistening pink, 

big as a basketball. The mouse caught by its tail in the trap I placed in the pantry, 

about which I knew nothing until my children asked me, Daddy, what’s that 

awful sound? Yet the squirrels dart across the road in front of speeding cars and 

miraculously dodge all the tires. Or not, in which case I find them flattened 

into crow pickings and eventually dried into pelts, but somehow the injured  

or disabled manage to crawl away out of my sight. Not on this day. 

I’m not very fond of squirrels anyway, I think of them as fluffy-tailed rats. 

Most wild animals that thrive around people these days, rats, mice, pigeons, 

crows, yellow jackets, cockroaches, are pretty much annoying, to people, that is. 

Soon after I saw a squirrel scampering away with crust of toast in its mouth, 

I had to replace our plastic garbage cans with galvanized steel ones, despite 

their tendency to rust and dent, because squirrels chewed right through 

the lids and rims of the plastic. Next, I had to change my habit 

of leaving my car windows open a crack in the summer sun, because 

squirrels chewed through the rubber weather stripping, in their attempt 

to get at the Cheerios and other food debris littering the backseat car seats. 

Then, while sitting on the couch in our remodeled cathedral-ceiling 

great room, I happened to notice out of the side of my eye a beady black 

eyeball winking at me from a squirrel’s head peering over the edge 

of the interior soffit, like it was a peanut gallery, or, after I had fetched 

 
the stepladder and climbed up to take a look, an acorn balcony. The builder 

had neglected to seal a narrow gap outside where the fascia met the stucco.  

So I had in for squirrels. 

Nonetheless, while I watched this one wind its pathetic and spastic way 

across the yard, I thought, why should this squirrel bear the brunt of 

my hostility for its species, since it appeared to be suffering enough already, 

and I doubted my resolve to carry out the decapitation or whatever 

bloody demise I could inflict with the tip of a shovel, having never killed 

a warm blooded creature before. Reflecting back on this episode recently, 

in light of personal experience, I considered if the explanation might be 

that the afflicted squirrel was having a stroke. After researching the topic, 

I learned that pets such as dogs and cats indeed have strokes, and in fact 

rabbits are commonly used as research subjects in stroke treatments, 

although I didn’t read anything about how to induce a stroke in a rabbit. 

In any case, it sounds like any animal with a brain supplied with blood 

can have a stroke, including squirrels. Symptoms of stroke, at least in dogs, 

include staggering, falling, circling, rolling, inability to get up, and 

eyes flicking back and forth (nystagmus, or squirrely eyes). The squirrel 

in the backyard certainly fit the profile. 

I wonder what would have happened last year if a different species of creature, 

larger, stronger, regarding themselves superior to me, would have chanced 

upon me lying on my back on the carpet of the room I use for a home gym, 

my right leg and arm inert, my body circling around on the floor like a moth 

with a missing wing, unable to get up, unable to make sound, my eyes 

searching the ceiling for solace when periodically I stopped struggling 

 
 
 
to right myself and gave in my exhaustion and desperation.  

Would the towering creature have put me out of my misery  

with a bullet to my brain, or a shovel blade to my neck? Would I have 

welcomed that?  

That day in the yard, I decided to let nature take its course, mostly I suppose 

out of cowardice rather than out of kindness, and went about my business, 

leaving the squirrel to thrash on the lawn. By next the morning, 

there was no sign of him. Some years later, on the floor, my wife found me 

after an hour, and called 911.
